## What Cross-Polarized Crystal Macro Is

Dissolve citric acid in water, smear a drop across a microscope slide, and let it dry. What grows is a colorless crust that looks like nothing under ordinary light. Photograph that slide through a macro lens between two polarizing filters set at right angles, and the crust becomes fields of saturated color on pure black — feathered dendrites in magenta and green, radial bursts of orange and cyan.

The color is real, but it is not in the chemical. The optics create it, and it encodes physical information about the crystal — mostly its thickness. Every variable you control on the bench shows up directly in the picture, which is why this genre rewards method over luck. It is also cheap: microscope slides, sheet polarizing film, compounds sold as food additives and garden chemicals, a lens that reaches somewhere near 1:1, and a light source you probably already own. What it demands instead of money is care in two places — preparing the slides, and aligning the optics.

## Why Crossed Polarizers Produce Color

A polarizing filter passes light vibrating in one plane. Cross two of them at 90° and no light gets through, so the background of every frame is black by construction. The interesting part happens when a crystal sits in the gap. Most crystals are birefringent — they split incoming light into two rays that travel at different speeds, so one exits delayed relative to the other. That delay is called retardation, and the second filter (the analyzer) recombines the rays so they interfere: some wavelengths cancel, others reinforce, and white light comes out colored. Which color depends on the retardation:

*retardation (nm) = thickness (µm) × 1000 × birefringence*

Birefringence is fixed for a given compound, so for any one chemical, **thickness is the color**. The mapping is the Michel-Lévy sequence: below about 200 nm of retardation, weak silvery greys; from roughly 200 to 1100 nm, saturated yellows, magentas, and blues; past about 1650 nm, colors that fade with each order into a blank creamy white. For the organics below, the saturated band means a film a few microns thick.

Everything else follows from that relationship. Each color band in the frame is a contour line of equal thickness — the photograph is a topographic map of the film, and the map can be authored. It also means a big grown crystal is the wrong subject: a centimeter-scale copper sulfate crystal sits hundreds of orders past white, and its familiar blue is absorption color, not interference. And not everything works — table salt is cubic, so it is not birefringent and stays black, as does unstressed glass.

## Choosing a Compound

The main thing to know: these compounds do not share a preparation method, and treating them alike wastes the session.

- **Citric acid** — the reliable starter. Saturated in 1:1 distilled water and isopropyl alcohol, it dries into feathery fans within hours.
- **Urea** — the showpiece: long dendritic feathering few compounds match. It barely dissolves in alcohol, so use warm distilled water with a splash of alcohol.
- **Ascorbic acid** — circular spherulites with dark Maltese crosses. Water-based, saturated.
- **Salol (phenyl salicylate)** — arguably the best. It melts at 41 to 43 °C, which a low-watt mug warmer holds, and recrystallizes while you watch, on a slide you can re-melt indefinitely.
- **Benzoic acid** — needle sprays from hot solution, or ferns grown from vapor. Distinctive habits; read the warning first.
- **Copper sulfate** — skeletal blue dendrites. Solution growth only, never heated, and the only real hazards on this list.

<Callout type="warning">
**Copper sulfate** is harmful if swallowed, irritates skin and eyes, and is very toxic to aquatic life — it is sold as a pond algaecide. Safety glasses whenever powder or solution is moving, nitrile gloves, and a dedicated cup, pipette, and stirrer that never return to food use. Never pour it down a drain — let spent solution evaporate in a labeled dish and reuse it — and never heat it, which drives off its water of crystallization and destroys both color and structure. **Benzoic acid** has a flash point of 121 °C, below its 122.4 °C melting point, so melting it necessarily passes the flash point. At match-head quantities the risk is vapor, not explosion, but the rules stand: electric heat only, no open flame, a ventilated room, no overshooting past about 130 °C — and prefer the sublimation route near 100 °C, which stays below the flash point. The rest — citric, urea, ascorbic, salol — are ordinary mild irritants: gloves, glasses, and ventilation when melting anything organic.
</Callout>

## Preparing Slides

### The solution method

Make a saturated solution (stir until a few grains stay undissolved), smear a drop thinly across a slide, and leave it open to the air for a few hours. No coverslip — evaporation is what grows the crystals, and a coverslip smothers it.

The lever that dominates is the solvent ratio: more alcohol, faster evaporation, finer feathering; more water, slower drying, blockier crystals. The smear matters just as much: a fat unsmeared drop dries to hundreds of microns at the center — dead chalky white, with color only in a ring near the rim — while a thin smear dries as a taper whose middle band carries the picture. (Copper sulfate films also lose water to dry air and go chalky — shoot them the day you make them.)

### The melt method

This is salol's method, and it turns a wait-hours process into minutes per attempt. Warm a slide to around 42 °C, add a few grains — a match-head, far less than instinct suggests — and lay a coverslip on once it melts. Then press.

The press is the point. The coverslip is a thickness control and the color is its gauge: silvery grey means too thin, add a grain; chalky pastel or creamy white means too thick, press harder or start with less. Strong first- and second-order color means you are in the live band. That closed loop — press until the colors arrive — is the whole technique.

One failure mode to expect: salol supercools and is a known glass-former, so a melt can simply refuse to crystallize, setting into a clear glass that reads dead black under crossed polarizers.

<Callout type="tip">
Keep a seed jar — a few grains of solid salol in a clean lid. When a melt sits glassy, touch one grain to the edge of the film with a toothpick and crystallization sweeps across from there. Seeding doubles as composition: where you touch is where the burst begins. And a vitrified slide is never wasted; re-melt it and go again.
</Callout>

### Hot solution and vapor

Benzoic acid gets two routes. Dissolve it in near-boiling water, pipette hot onto a slide, and needles strike out as it cools — concentration acts as a shape dial, from needles through plates to hexagons. Or warm the powder to around 100 °C with a cool slide suspended above it, and ferns grow onto the glass straight from the vapor, a habit nothing else in the cupboard produces.

### Authoring the pattern

The failure mode in this genre is not a blank slide. It is an even one — a uniform field of pretty texture with nothing happening in it. The pattern is decided at the bench, so author it there:

- **Scratch the glass** where you want growth to start. A line grows fans along the line. It feels like cheating and is actually composition.
- **Leave gaps.** Drops separated by clean glass read pure black, and the black frames the color. A flooded slide has no negative space.
- **Let two drops just touch.** The seam where the drying fronts collide is a hard boundary with different structure on each side — a built-in gesture.
- **Wedge the coverslip** on a melt by propping one edge on a sliver of glass. Thickness ramps smoothly across the film, and since thickness is color, one slide carries the whole color sequence.

Change one variable per slide, label the frosted end, and keep a one-line log — twelve slides that each differ by one thing are a reference chart you will use for years.

## The Cross-Polarized Setup

The optical sandwich, in order: light source, polarizer, slide, analyzer, lens. Build it vertically, lens pointing down, slide lying flat above the light — a flat slide needs no holder, and a molten film on a vertical slide runs.

The cheapest good source is a screen: most LCD displays emit linearly polarized light, so a tablet or monitor showing a full-white image is a ready-made polarized backlight. Test it first — hold your polarizing filter to the white screen and rotate it. It must go fully black at some angle, not just dim. Turn off auto-brightness and any color-shifting mode, or exposure will drift between frames. The alternative is a lamp or flash behind a diffuser with linear polarizing film laid on top — on top, because diffusers depolarize and film underneath gives no polarized light at all.

For the analyzer, a circular polarizing filter on the lens works: rotate until the background goes black (extinction). Rotation changes the colors, never the focus — focus at a bright rotation, then rotate to taste. If the lens has no filter thread, gut a cheap screw-in filter, drop in a disc of linear polarizing film, and screw the retaining ring back down; the ring tensions the film flat, and wrinkled film shows as patches that never quite reach black. Stand it in the gap near the lens, tilted a few degrees to stop reflections.

Two checks gate every session. Black out the room. Then the contamination test: source set to black, one frame at shooting settings — it must come back pure black, because washed-out crystal color is almost never chemistry, it is a light leak. For a first subject that cannot fail, layer strips of clear tape on a spare slide: tape is birefringent, and it lets you learn extinction, rotation, and exposure with zero stakes while the slides dry.

## Getting the Slide Parallel to the Sensor

At these magnifications depth of field is measured in microns, and your subject is a flat film. If the slide is not parallel to the sensor, only a strip of it crosses the plane of focus, and moving focus just slides the sharp band across the frame. The tolerance — roughly depth of field divided by frame width — is fractions of a degree.

<ToolCard slug="dof-simulator" />

The instinctive fix — tilting the camera to match the slide — fails geometrically. The head pivots well behind the subject, so a fraction-of-a-degree correction swings the focal plane through many full depths of field: every adjustment throws the subject out of focus, and refocusing tells you nothing about whether the tilt improved. Adjust the slide instead.

Better still, let the lens do the measuring. The front rim of a lens is machined perpendicular to the optical axis, and so is the sensor — so the lens's front face is permanently parallel to the sensor, and anything pressed flat against it is too. Rest a small rigid plate on two lumps of non-hardening modeling clay and lower the lens gently onto it. The high edge touches first, its clay compresses more, and the plate rotates until it sits flush — a rigid plate cannot be both flush against a flat face and tilted relative to it, so the error squeezes itself out. Raise the lens; the clay holds the plate where the lens left it, and a slide laid on it is parallel to your sensor. This levels to the lens, not to gravity: desk, tripod, and head can all be crooked. For backlit work, cut a hole in the plate and let the slide bridge it.

Four details decide whether it works. Press against a sacrificial filter or a flat cap, never the front element. The rig must be rigid — anything that flexes while you press levels the plate to the flexed geometry, wrong the moment you let go. Use non-hardening plasticine; doughs dry and crack, soft putties creep within the hour. And a glass platform must be low-stress glass — test it between your crossed polarizers and reject it unless it stays black. Tempered glass never passes.

Then verify: punch live view to 100% and check that all four corners are sharp at the same focus position — a soft corner names the direction you are still tilted. Check at low magnification first, where errors are easy to see.

## Capture and Focus Stacking

Expect long exposures: close focus costs about two stops of light at 1:1, the polarizers take another two to three, and frames near extinction commonly run to whole seconds on a screen backlight. Base ISO, stabilization off, remote release or self-timer, hands off everything during the exposure — a touched analyzer is a blurred frame. Shoot raw with fixed white balance so a rotation series stays comparable.

Most slides here need exactly one frame. A pressed melt film or a dried smear is a plane a few microns deep, inside a depth of field that covers it several times over. If a flat slide comes back soft, the cause is tilt, not depth — re-level rather than stack, because stacking a tilted plane just launders the alignment error into forty frames.

Stacking earns its place on subjects with real depth: the raised rim of a dried drop, vapor-grown ferns standing off the glass, anything approaching a millimeter. Each focus step must be smaller than the critically sharp depth of one frame so the sharp bands overlap, with two or three padding frames past sharp at each end. And prefer to stack at lower magnification when framing allows — depth of field per slice grows quickly as magnification drops, and cropping buys the framing back.

One habit specific to this genre: shoot every keeper at three analyzer rotations. Rotation sweeps the dark extinction bands across the field while the color bands stay put — the dark is positionable, and three rotations of one field are three different pictures, not three exposures of one.

Finally, remember what the preparation was for. The compositions that work have a nameable gesture: a growth front advancing into black, a burst placed off-center, the seam where two fronts collided, one dark fracture crossing a color field. If you cannot name the gesture before firing, you are photographing texture — and texture at any resolution is still texture. Keep the black black, keep one gesture per frame, and leave with twelve considered frames instead of two hundred rotations of the same field.
