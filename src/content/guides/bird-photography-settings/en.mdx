## Start From a Working Baseline

Bird photography punishes indecision. The subject is small, it moves without warning, and the second you spend fumbling a dial is the second the bird leaves. Here is a baseline that covers most of a field session on any current camera:

- Manual mode, with Auto ISO carrying the exposure
- Shutter speed set for the bird's motion: around 1/500s for a perched bird, 1/2500s for flight
- Aperture wide open
- Continuous autofocus with subject or eye detection switched on
- Exposure compensation as the one control you ride as conditions change

The rest of this guide is why each piece is set that way, when to break from it, and the fieldcraft settings can't replace. A camera can freeze a wingbeat at 1/3200s. It cannot make the bird take off toward you in good light.

## Shutter Speed for Perched Birds

The common mistake with perched birds is calculating shutter speed from your own hands. Stabilized long lenses handle photographer shake down to surprisingly slow speeds; what actually limits you is the bird. An alert bird is never still: head turns, throat pulses, tail flicks, feather shivers, all of it blurring at speeds your stabilizer copes with easily. Around 1/400s to 1/500s handles an alert bird.

The exception is worth real money in ISO: some birds genuinely freeze. A heron stalking fish holds statue-still for long stretches, and during those beats you can drop to 1/125s and bank two full stops of light. The skill is telling frozen from about-to-move, and that comes from watching birds, not screens.

Your own handholding limit still matters once stabilization is off, or at focal lengths long enough to defeat it. Know where that floor sits for your focal length before a bird is in front of you.

## Shutter Speed for Birds in Flight

Flight is a different problem and wants a different number: 1/2000s to 1/3200s as the default. Large birds gliding can hold together at 1/1600s. Small fast flyers like swallows and terns want 1/3200s or beyond. When in doubt, go faster.

Freeze the bird with shutter speed, not stabilization. Stabilization steadies your framing and does nothing whatsoever about the bird's motion, which is a common and expensive confusion.

Panning matters as much as the number. Swing from the torso with your feet planted rather than steering with your arms, and keep panning through the burst and past it, the way a golfer follows through. Stopping the pan as you press is what smears wingtips at shutter speeds that should have been fast enough.

There's also a deliberately slow option: 1/250s to 1/500s with a smooth pan keeps the head sharp while the wingtips blur into motion. It fails more often than it works, and the frames it produces feel alive in a way frozen ones sometimes don't. For a big swirling flock, drop all the way to 1/15s to 1/60s and let the motion be the picture.

## Aperture: Wide Open Is Usually Right

This is the easiest setting in the genre, because the lens mostly decides it for you. Telephoto lenses with the reach for birds typically open to somewhere between f/4.5 and f/6.3, and you'll shoot at that maximum almost all the time.

Light is scarce: the best bird activity and the best light both come in the first hours of the day, and every third of a stop you give up comes back as ISO. Depth of field at bird distances already covers the subject: at 600mm and f/6.3 with a duck fifteen meters out, the sharp zone spans the bird, so stopping down to f/8 buys depth you didn't need at an ISO cost you'll notice. Lenses in this class also tend to perform at or near their best wide open. And a wide aperture at long focal length melts the background, which is most of what separates a bird photograph from a bird record.

The exceptions: two birds you want sharp together, a bird close enough that depth of field collapses, or the environmental frame, where a small silhouette sits in a big scene at sunset. That last one is really a landscape photo that contains a bird; shoot it like one, at f/8 to f/13 from a tripod.

## Focal Length: What 400–600mm Buys You

The working range for birds on a full-frame camera runs from 400mm to 600mm, and shorter reach has a real cost. As a rough rule, filling most of the frame takes a focal length around thirty times the ratio of distance to bird size. In practice, a sparrow-sized bird fills a 600mm frame only inside about three meters, a duck at around twelve, a heron past twenty. With 400mm, cut those distances by a third; with 300mm, halve them. Every meter you have to close is fieldcraft you have to execute and disturbance you risk.

Flight is more forgiving than the perched numbers suggest, because a flying bird is framed by its wingspan rather than its body, and wingspan runs about one and a half times body length for most waterbirds and songbirds, two to three times for raptors. The same heron that needs 600mm perched can be framed in flight at 400mm.

High-resolution sensors stretch the range further. Cropping a 45-megapixel frame by 1.5x still leaves roughly 20 megapixels and frames like 900mm; a crop-sensor body does the same thing in hardware. Teleconverters trade differently: a 1.4x converter buys real optical reach but costs a stop of light and some autofocus speed, which makes it a tool for static birds in good light rather than a default.

## Auto ISO With a Shutter Floor

The exposure recipe that makes all of the above workable: set shutter speed and aperture manually, and let Auto ISO carry the exposure. You've made the two decisions that actually shape the image, motion and depth, and delegated the one that noise reduction can partially undo later. A sharp bird at ISO 6400 is a photograph. A blurred bird at ISO 800 is a deletion.

If you prefer aperture priority, the same idea lives inside the Auto ISO settings as a minimum shutter speed. Set that floor at your perched or flight number and the camera holds it, raising ISO instead of slowing down. Either way, exposure compensation still works on most current bodies by biasing the Auto ISO, and it becomes the control you actively ride as light and backgrounds change.

## Metering: White Feathers, Dark Plumage, Bright Sky

The meter tries to render everything as middle grey, and most birds worth photographing aren't grey. The correction direction comes from the background, not the bird.

A white bird on dark water, an egret or a swan, is the classic clipping trap. The meter exposes for the dark surroundings and the whites blow to paper. Pull back a third to a full stop. This failure is unforgiving in one direction only: a slightly dark white bird lifts cleanly in raw, but feather texture clipped to white is gone and no slider brings it back.

A dark bird against bright water or sky fails the opposite way. The meter guards the bright background and leaves the bird a detail-less shadow, so open up a third to a full stop and let the background go bright. Any bird against open sky needs added light for the same reason, and so do snow and fog, both of which read bright and fool the meter dark.

If your camera offers highlight-weighted metering, know its blind spot: it protects the brightest thing in the frame. That's exactly right when the bird is the brightest thing, the egret on dark water, and exactly wrong when a bright sky outshines the bird. Use it for white-on-dark and trust the histogram everywhere else.

Backlight can also be the goal. Expose for the bright sky, let the bird fall to black, and you have a silhouette, which works when the contour is legible. Herons, egrets, and anything with a long neck and a dagger bill read instantly. A resting duck reads as a lump.

## Autofocus: Detection Helps, Setup Decides

Subject detection has changed bird photography more than any lens has, but it hasn't removed the decisions. It has moved them.

For flight: continuous AF, bird or animal detection on, and an area mode chosen by the background. When the bird separates cleanly against sky, a tracking mode that follows the subject anywhere in the frame works well. Against clutter such as a treeline, reeds, or a busy shoreline, shrink to a medium zone so the system has less background to be tempted by, and start the lock while the bird is still against open sky if you can. Most bodies also expose a tracking-stickiness setting; run it high for flight so a crossing branch or a second bird doesn't steal the lock. The caveat cuts both ways: if the system has grabbed the background, high stickiness makes it hold that mistake just as stubbornly. Clean acquisition matters more than any menu setting.

For perched birds, do less. A single point on the eye, placed on a contrast edge like the junction of eye and bill rather than a smooth patch of feathers, and either single-shot AF or a locked back-button hold so the camera doesn't re-hunt between frames.

<Callout type="warning">
Detection is not focus. The eye-detection box can ride the bird perfectly while the focus drive itself sits on the branch behind it, so the display shows a hit and the card shows a miss. Check your first frames at magnification early in a session rather than discovering this at home.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
Move autofocus off the shutter button and onto a rear button under your thumb. Hold it to track a bird in flight; release it to lock focus on a perched bird and recompose. One configuration then covers both situations, and the shutter button just takes pictures.
</Callout>

If your lens has a focus limiter switch, set it to the distant range and leave it there. Nearly every bird is beyond the near limit anyway, and the limiter turns a lost lock into an instant re-grab instead of a full-travel hunt to minimum focus and back — often the difference between catching and missing a bird that flushes.

## Burst, Buffer, and Cards

A flight sequence at fifteen to thirty frames per second is partly a wing-position lottery, and the burst buys tickets. The frames that read are the top of the upstroke and the full downbeat, where the wing shape is unmistakable; mid-stroke frames hide the body behind the near wing. Whatever the wings are doing, a flight shot with no visible eye and no catchlight is a record rather than a photograph, so cull accordingly.

Sustained bursts are where buffers and cards stop being spec-sheet trivia. A camera that stalls mid-burst because the buffer filled is a camera that missed the strike. Buffer depth and card write speed matter more for birds than headline frame rate, so test how long your body sustains its burst before a morning that counts. Some bodies also offer pre-capture, which buffers frames from before the full shutter press. That's useful for staked-out takeoffs, though it often records in JPEG or reduced quality rather than full raw.

## Fieldcraft: The Part the Camera Can't Do

Every setting above can be right and the photo still fails, because the things that decide it most were set with your feet.

Light direction first. The reliable default is low sun behind you, your own shadow pointing at the bird, which lights the subject evenly and puts a catchlight in the eye. Side light rakes across feathers and shows texture at the cost of a shadowed far side. Backlight makes rim-lit wings and silhouettes. Overhead midday light is the one to avoid: flat color, shadowed eye sockets, harsh undersides. This is most of why serious bird photography happens at sunrise. The exception: an overcast sky is a giant diffuser and the kindest light there is for white birds, which hold feather detail instead of clipping.

Height is background control. From standing, your sightline passes over the bird and lands on ground a few meters behind it: near, busy, and inside the depth of field. Kneel to the bird's eye level and the same sightline lands tens of meters back, where a wide aperture melts it smooth. The image also stops looking like a specimen viewed from above and starts looking like the bird's world. At long focal lengths the background is a sliver of the scene, so one step sideways can swap a blown-out sky gap for dark foliage. Pick the background first, then wait for the bird to give you the head angle.

Approach low, slow, and at an angle rather than straight on, and drop your height before you close distance. When the bird stops feeding to watch you, stop moving. That stare is the last warning before it flushes.

Anticipation beats reaction, because at long focal lengths you can't react to something that has already happened. Perched birds telegraph takeoff: a crouch, a forward lean, a lifted tail, defecation, a wing flick. Pre-focus during the tells and you catch the launch instead of an empty perch. Birds take off and land into the wind, so note the wind direction when you arrive and position so it works for you. Flight paths repeat: birds follow shorelines, treelines, and ridgelines. Watch a pass or two, find the lane, and wait there instead of chasing. Landings beat takeoffs: an incoming bird decelerates, flares, and spreads everything, giving you a slower subject at its most photogenic.

<Callout type="warning">
A flushed bird is a worse photograph and a stressed animal, and the failures compound: flushed birds learn to keep more distance. The long lens exists so you can photograph from a distance the bird tolerates, not so you can push closer. If your presence is changing the bird's behavior — it has stopped feeding, it's staring, it's edging away — you are already too close. Back off and let the bird come to you. It often will.
</Callout>

The baseline at the top of this guide takes an afternoon to learn. The fieldcraft takes seasons, and it's the better investment: settings determine whether the shot is sharp, but fieldcraft determines whether there's a shot at all.
