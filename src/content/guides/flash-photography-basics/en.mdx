## Why Flash Confuses Almost Everyone at First

Every flash photograph is two exposures stacked in a single frame. One is the ambient exposure, made by whatever light already exists in the scene. The other is the flash exposure, made by the burst your flash adds. The sensor records both during one shutter cycle, but they answer to different controls, and nothing on the camera tells you which belongs to which.

That split is why flash feels random when you start. Raise the shutter speed and the background goes dark while your subject stays exactly as bright. Stop down the aperture and everything darkens at once. Step a pace closer with the flash and the subject blows out while the room doesn't move. Each of those results follows a rule; they just belong to two different exposures, and until you separate the two in your head, cause and effect never line up.

The rest of this guide is that separation: which control moves which exposure, how to balance them, what sync speed limits, when to use TTL or manual, and why distance, size, and direction matter more than power.

## Which Control Moves Which Exposure

What makes flash controllable is buried in the timing. A flash burst is short: roughly 1/1,000 of a second at full power, and shorter still as you cut power. The shutter might stay open for 1/200 of a second, or half a second, or twenty seconds for a night exposure. The burst fits inside almost any shutter time with room to spare, so opening the shutter longer admits no additional flash. It only admits more of the ambient.

Shutter speed touches the ambient exposure only, as long as you stay at or below sync speed (covered below). Flash power and flash-to-subject distance touch the flash exposure only. Aperture and ISO touch both at once.

That makes shutter speed the lever for the background, power and distance the levers for the subject, and aperture and ISO the overall level, which drags both exposures and forces a rebalance whenever it moves.

## Ambient First, Then Flash

The working method is two steps, always in the same order.

Set the ambient with the flash off. Expose the scene the way you want the background to look. For a subject you plan to light, that is often one to two stops darker than a "correct" exposure, so the flash reads as the main light instead of competing with the scene. For gentle fill in daylight it might be a normal exposure. Decide the background first, because no flash setting will move it.

Then turn the flash on and set its level, with power and distance in manual or with flash exposure compensation in TTL, until the subject sits right against the background you already chose.

That order is also what makes slow-shutter flash work at night. Set 1/15 or 1/30 in a lit street and the ambient smears: headlights streak and signs drag into ribbons. The flash burst still lasts its fraction of a millisecond, so whatever it reaches is frozen sharp inside the blur. The burst behaves like a second, much faster shutter that applies only to the flash-lit parts of the frame. Any blur on the subject comes from ambient light continuing to expose them during the rest of the shutter time.

<Callout type="tip">
When you drag the shutter on a moving subject, set the flash to rear-curtain sync so the burst fires at the end of the exposure instead of the beginning. Motion trails then fall behind the direction of travel. Front-curtain sync puts the streak in front, and the subject appears to move backward.
</Callout>

## Sync Speed, and What High-Speed Sync Buys

There is one hard mechanical limit. A focal-plane shutter, the kind in nearly every interchangeable-lens camera, exposes the whole sensor at once only up to a certain speed, usually between 1/160 and 1/250, listed in your camera's specifications as the flash sync speed. Above it, the second curtain starts closing before the first has finished opening, and the exposure becomes a narrow slit traveling across the sensor. A flash burst fired into that lights only where the slit happens to be; the rest of the frame gets a hard black band.

High-speed sync (HSS) is the workaround. Instead of one burst, the flash pulses continuously while the slit travels, so every row of the sensor sees flash light. It works up to 1/8000 on most systems, and costs roughly two stops of effective power, because much of the pulsed output never reaches the sensor.

That cost makes HSS a trade, and one situation justifies it: a wide aperture in bright light. An f/2 portrait against a sunlit sky needs a shutter speed far past sync, so you spend the two stops. At night the question rarely comes up; your shutter speeds sit under the ceiling anyway, and flashes without HSS are no handicap after dark.

<Callout type="warning">
If the flash refuses to fire, check the shutter mode first. Silent modes, fully electronic shutter, and high-speed burst modes disable flash entirely on many cameras, and the camera will not always tell you that is why.
</Callout>

## TTL or Manual

TTL and manual get argued as camps. Choose per situation; each is clearly better at something.

In TTL, the flash fires a brief metering pre-flash, the camera reads what comes back and sets the power for the real burst automatically. You steer the result with flash exposure compensation (FEC), in stops. TTL earns its keep when flash-to-subject distance changes frame to frame, as at events, where subjects drift toward and away faster than you could re-meter. Start around minus one stop of FEC when the flash is an accent, zero when it is the main light.

Most systems also offer a flash value lock, which freezes the TTL reading while you recompose, so the meter stops second-guessing every framing change.

In manual you set the power yourself as a fraction of full output: 1/1, 1/2, down to 1/128 on most units. Nothing re-decides between frames. The moment your working distance is fixed — a flash on a stand, a subject on a mark — manual is easier, not harder, because the exposure cannot drift. Adjust in third-stop steps and judge by the histogram.

Two habits keep either mode honest. Set FEC in one place only; compensation dialed on the camera body and on a trigger stack together, so pick one and leave the other at zero. And FEC does nothing in manual mode. There is no automated answer to compensate; you are the answer, so change the power itself.

## Distance Is Power: The Inverse-Square Law

Light from a flash falls off with the square of distance. Double the flash-to-subject distance and a quarter of the light arrives, a loss of two stops. Halve the distance and you gain two. That one relationship does a surprising amount of flash craft.

Up close, small movements swing exposure hard: moving a flash from one meter to 1.4 meters costs a full stop. At longer range the same step barely registers. This is why close handheld flash demands attention and fixed setups are so stable in manual.

Falloff is also a background control. Put the subject one meter from the flash with a wall three meters behind them, and the wall receives about three stops less light: near black, in any room, without touching a setting. Pull the flash further back so the two distances are more alike and the background returns. You place darkness by choosing distances.

Guide numbers put usable figures on flash power. A guide number is distance multiplied by f-number for a correct full-power exposure at ISO 100, so a flash with a guide number of 30 in meters exposes correctly at three meters and f/10. In practice you will judge by the histogram, but guide numbers are the honest way to compare flashes. As rough classes, hot-shoe flashes deliver around 50 to 100 watt-seconds of equivalent output; studio-style monolights run from about 200 up past 600. The extra power is not about a brighter frame, since you set the level either way. It is headroom for light you deliberately throw away on modifiers, bounce surfaces, and HSS.

Because flash exposure is set with aperture and ISO, lighting recipes do not transfer directly across sensor formats. ## Soft Light Is Big Light, Measured From the Subject

Shadow softness is controlled by one thing: how large the light source appears from where the subject stands. Not how large the modifier measures on its box, and not how powerful the flash is. The sun is enormous and still produces hard light because it is far away. A modest softbox produces genuinely soft light, but only close in.

A useful rule of thumb: a source starts reading as soft when its width is comparable to its distance from the subject. A 30-centimeter softbox is soft at half a meter and effectively a point source at three meters. That gap explains a lot of disappointing small-modifier portraits; the light was soft in the product photos and hard at the distance it was actually used.

Bounce beats most modifiers indoors. Tilt the flash head at a white ceiling or wall and the lit patch becomes the source, often a couple of meters across, wrapping the subject in near-shadowless light. It costs two to three stops, exactly the headroom larger flashes exist to provide. At the other end of the scale, a bare flash 30 centimeters from a flower is enormous relative to the subject, which is why close-up flash can look soft with no modifier at all.

Choose modifiers by size and diffusion, not by name. Small clip-on domes mostly spread light around the room, which helps bounce but does little to soften direct flash at portrait distances. Umbrellas give a large source cheaply and spill everywhere; softboxes cost more but control where the light goes; bare reflectors make deliberately hard, raking light for texture. Every one of them answers to the same question: how big does it look from where the subject stands?

## Get the Flash Off the Camera

A flash in the hot shoe, aimed forward, lights the subject from the lens axis. Shadows fall directly behind whatever you shoot, faces flatten, and the frame takes on the familiar direct-flash look: flat and a little harsh, fine for fill or documentary work, wrong for anything you want shaped.

Move the light even 45 degrees off the lens axis and the subject acquires form. The standard one-light starting position is 45 degrees to the side and 45 degrees up, aimed down at the subject. From there, walk the light in small steps and watch the nose shadow move until the face looks dimensional. Then feather: aim the soft edge of the light's cone at the subject instead of the hot center, which evens the falloff across a face.

The mechanics are simpler than they sound. A radio trigger sits in the hot shoe and fires a receiver built into most current flashes; a light stand with a tilting bracket holds the flash and a modifier. Failing all that, holding the flash at arm's length on a trigger or sync cord is a real technique: one step off axis does more for a portrait than any amount of added power. Direction beats power, nearly every time.

## A First Practice Hour

Three drills, one evening, one small object, one flash. Each isolates one idea so you feel it instead of memorizing it.

First, the ladder. Lock the camera in manual (something like ISO 400, f/4, 1/200) and put the flash exactly one meter from a mug. Shoot the full power range, 1/64 up to 1/2, one frame per step. Then lock the power at 1/16 and step the distance instead: one meter, 1.4, two, 2.8. Afterward you know what a stop of flash looks like from both ends.

Second, kill the room. Same scene, room lights on. Find camera settings that render the room black with no flash, bring the mug back with flash alone, then let the room return one shutter stop at a time, 1/200 to 1/100 to 1/50, watching the background rise while the mug holds steady. That is ambient/flash balance as a repeatable exercise.

Third, the clock face. Put the light on a subject (a willing friend, or anything with a nose-like protrusion) at fixed power and distance, and walk it around them: on axis, 45 degrees, 90, then behind for rim light. One frame per position. You are training your eye to see direction.

Every flash recipe you will ever read, from the dragged-shutter night frame to the softbox portrait, is a recombination of what these drills teach: what a stop of flash is, how ambient and flash balance, and what direction does.
