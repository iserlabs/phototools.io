## Correction and Grading Are Different Jobs

Color correction and color grading get treated as one job, but they answer different questions. Correction asks what the scene actually looked like: you neutralize casts, settle the exposure, and make skin believable. Done well, correction converges: ten careful editors land on roughly the same file. Grading asks what the picture should feel like, and it diverges by design. One editor pushes the shadows cool and lets the lamplight glow; another drains the greens and lifts the blacks toward gray. Both can be right, because a grade is a choice, not an answer.

The order matters. A grade applied over an uncorrected file inherits every mistake underneath it. If the image already carries a blue cast and you add a cool shadow tint on top, you have not built a look. You have deepened an accident, and every other color you place will sit on that tilted base. Correct first, so the colors you add land where you put them.

One more distinction before touching a slider. A grade is a decision about the relationships between colors, not about the quantity of color. The most common beginner mistake is treating "graded" as a synonym for "more": more saturation, more contrast, more of everything at once. That produces a loud photo, not a graded one, for reasons that come down to how the eye reads a picture.

## White Balance Is the Foundation

Much of what feels like a saturation problem is actually a cast problem. Neutralize the cast and the color you were about to add often turns out to be already there, hidden under a layer of blue or green. White balance therefore comes before any grading decision: it is the reference point you depart from, and you cannot depart deliberately from a point you never established.

White balance runs on two axes, not one. Temperature moves the image along a blue–amber line; tint moves it along a green–magenta line. They look independent, and they are not. Every color in the file is three numbers (red, green, blue), and warming an image raises red while lowering blue. Green barely moves. On a neutral image this is harmless. On a cool, cyan-leaning image, where blue and green start high and red starts low, a big warm move drops blue down to meet the rising red and leaves green sitting on top. The photo does not turn golden. It turns olive. This is why large temperature moves usually need tint pushed toward magenta at the same time: the second axis compensates for the channel the first one never touches.

Once neutrals are actually neutral, white balance becomes a grading control in its own right. A warm evening look is often nothing more than a corrected file nudged a few hundred kelvin warmer, plus a little magenta tint to hold the greens in check. The difference between that and an uncorrected warm cast is intent: one departs from a known neutral, the other was never neutral to begin with. Viewers cannot name the difference, but they see it.

## Why Loud Edits Read as Loud

Photographs work through differences. A bright area against a dim one, a saturated subject among duller surroundings: contrasts like these are the path the eye follows through the frame. Global saturation and global contrast both spend those differences. Each takes the full spread of values and pushes it toward the extremes, which leaves fewer distinguishable steps than you started with. Push everything up together and the relationships between areas mostly survive, because the eye measures areas against each other, not against an absolute scale. What you lose is range. Highlights merge, shadow detail fuses into black, and strongly colored areas flatten into single-value patches.

Saturation has a specific failure mode worth knowing. Each of the three channels stops at its own ceiling, and they do not arrive together. Keep pushing and the first channel to clip stops contributing, which changes the ratio between the survivors — and the ratio is the hue. Oranges slide toward red, skin picks up a brick tint, and greens turn harsh. A slider labeled saturation quietly changes which color things are. At the same time, the tonal variation inside each color, the shading that makes a surface look rounded and lit, compresses toward a single value, so subjects turn into flat colored shapes.

The two sliders also feed each other. Add contrast and colors appear more saturated; add saturation and tones appear punchier. The familiar back-and-forth between the two ends up applying each one roughly twice, without anyone deciding to.

Your starting point may already be graded. Phone HDR and the default profile a camera applies to its JPEGs add contrast and saturation before you touch anything, and a display running a vivid picture mode adds more on top. If every edit seems to need winding back the next day, check what the pipeline and the screen are adding before concluding the file needs to be louder.
</Callout>

And your eye drifts while you work. Spend half an hour in a file and your visual system adapts to what is on screen; the edit stops registering, so you push further to feel the same effect. That is normal adaptation, not weak discipline, and the fix is procedural: step away, then look at the image again cold before exporting. Edits that survive the next morning are usually quieter than the ones that felt right at midnight.

## Hue, Saturation, and Luminance Are Three Separate Moves

Every color adjustment is one of three moves: changing which color something is (hue), how far it sits from gray (saturation), or how bright it is (luminance). Most editors expose all three per color band in a color mixer. Lightroom and Camera Raw call the panel HSL; Capture One calls it the Color Editor. The controls are the same.

Beginners reach for saturation because it is the most visible of the three, yet it is usually the weakest tool for the job. A pale sky rarely needs more blue saturation; darkening the blue luminance deepens it the way a polarizer would, without pushing it toward the crayon version. Foliage that looks wrong is usually a hue problem, greens leaning too yellow or too cyan, and no amount of saturation fixes a wrong hue. It amplifies it.

The mixer also matters for a structural reason: it selects by color, while split toning selects by brightness. These are different selection axes, and choosing the right one is half of grading. If the thing you want to move is "everything orange" (skin, lamplight, brick), a hue-targeted control reaches exactly those areas and nothing else. If the thing you want to move is "the brightest part of the frame," a tone-targeted control does that regardless of what color it is. Trouble starts when the tool's selection axis does not match your intent, and split toning is where that mismatch bites hardest.

## Color Relationships: Complementary and Analogous

A grade is easier to build when you know what palette you are building toward, and palettes hold together for reasons you can point at, none of them mystical.

Complementary schemes pair two hues from opposite sides of the color wheel. Teal against orange is the famous one, blue against gold close behind. Two things make the pairing work. The first is simultaneous contrast: a color appears more saturated next to its complement than it does alone, so both halves of the pair look richer than their measured values. You get apparent intensity without spending actual saturation. The second is that the warm–cool split maps onto how light behaves. Warm sources (sun, tungsten, firelight, skin) against cool ambient shadow is a pattern viewers have seen their whole lives, so a frame organized that way looks lit, not filtered. Teal-and-orange became a cliché precisely because it works: skin sits in the orange band, so pushing everything that is not skin toward the opposite hue makes people stand out from the background.

Analogous schemes take the opposite approach: three or four neighboring hues, golds through reds, or blues through greens, with no strong opposition anywhere. The result is a unified atmosphere instead of a subject-and-background split. With no complement to generate contrast, whatever small accent breaks the scheme carries enormous weight, and that is a tool in itself. One red element in a blue-green frame draws the eye harder than any saturation push could.

The working principle is the same in both cases. A grade usually reduces the number of distinct hues in a frame and organizes what remains, and that restraint is what makes the surviving colors look intentional.

## Split Toning: One Color for Shadows, Another for Highlights

Split toning tints the image by brightness band: one hue for the shadows, another for the highlights, and a balance control that decides where the crossover falls. Lightroom and Camera Raw now call the panel Color Grading; Capture One and most video tools present the same idea as three color wheels for shadows, midtones, and highlights. Same operation, different clothes.

It earns its place in a grade because it imitates something real. Mixed lighting splits along tone naturally. A subject lit by warm tungsten stands in shadows filled by cool skylight, so the warm–cool split follows the brightness split on its own. A gentle split tone with cool shadows and warm highlights, both at low saturation, reads as plausible light because it mirrors how actual scenes are lit.

Two cautions keep it honest. Saturation values stay low: a split tone is a bias, not a paint job, and the moment the tint is nameable at a glance it is usually too strong. And check what actually occupies each brightness band before assigning it a color. The highlight control tints whatever is brightest, and the brightest area is not always what you meant to warm. In a frame where the brightest zone is sky, a warm highlight tint does not make the light glow; it yellows the sky. When the warm and cool content of a scene is separated by hue rather than by brightness, the color mixer is the better instrument, and the split tone should only lay down a quiet base underneath it.

## Keep Skin and Neutrals Honest

Viewers carry expectations for a handful of familiar subjects: skin, gray pavement, white shirts, green grass. The standard term is memory colors, and the tolerance around each one varies. Skin is the least forgiving of them all. An over-pushed sunset can survive for a while, since no viewer knows what that particular sunset really looked like, but skin that has drifted toward brick or orange fails on sight.

This gives a graded image its anchor points. A frame can move a long way from reality and still look intentional, provided something in it stays honest. Keep skin inside its believable range and keep at least one genuine neutral (a white, a gray, a specular highlight) actually neutral, and the rest of the palette reads as a look built around trustworthy references. Let everything drift together and the same adjustments read as a cast.

In practice this means exempting skin from the bigger moves. Hue-targeted controls make that straightforward: skin lives in the orange band of the mixer, so hue rotations and saturation moves elsewhere in the frame can leave that band alone. When a global move has to pass through skin, such as a strong white balance departure, check faces before and after. They will show the damage before anything else does.

## A Working Order

Grades fail from sequence as often as from taste. An order that holds up:

1. Correct first. Neutral neutrals, settled exposure, believable skin. This is the reference everything departs from.
2. Decide the palette before moving sliders. Two or three hues, named out loud: cool shadows with amber light, or muted greens with one warm accent. A grade without a stated destination turns into an accumulation of small pushes.
3. Set the base with white balance, a deliberate departure from neutral, and move tint along with temperature.
4. Place color by hue in the mixer. Shift, mute, or darken individual bands until the frame's colors gather into the palette you named.
5. Lay the split tone over the top at low saturation, after checking what each brightness band actually contains.
6. Protect the anchors. Skin honest, one neutral true.
7. Walk away. Look again cold, at thumbnail size and at full size, before exporting.

The last step is the cheapest and most reliable, because of a simple asymmetry: you can always take a restrained grade further tomorrow, but there is no road back from a loud export. Once the channels have clipped and the shading inside the colors has flattened, that information is gone from the file. The quiet version leaves every door open.
