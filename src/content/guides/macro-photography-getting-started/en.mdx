## What Makes Macro Different

Most photography captures a scene. Macro photography captures a detail — the texture of a leaf, the compound eye of an insect, the grain in a piece of wood — at a scale large enough that the viewer sees structure the naked eye would normally miss. What separates macro from an ordinary close-up isn't a subject category; it's how large the subject renders on the sensor relative to its size in real life. That relationship has a name: magnification.

Because macro work happens at extreme close range, familiar variables behave differently. Depth of field collapses to fractions of a millimeter. Small camera movements become large framing shifts. Light falls off faster the closer you get. None of this requires specialized gear to understand — it requires understanding magnification, since magnification drives all the others.

## Magnification and Working Distance

Magnification is the ratio between a subject's size on the sensor and its size in the real world. A ratio of 1:1 — often called "true macro" or "life-size" — means a subject that is 24mm across in reality projects as a 24mm-wide image on the sensor. A ratio of 1:2 means it projects at half that size, and so on. Many lenses marketed for close-up work top out well short of 1:1; only lenses (or lens-plus-accessory combinations) that reach 1:1 or beyond are doing genuine macro reproduction.

### Why Focal Length Changes Working Distance

Working distance is the gap between the front of the lens and the subject at a given magnification — and it's easy to confuse with focal length, but the two aren't the same thing. For any two lenses reaching the same magnification, the one with the longer focal length will achieve it from farther away. A short focal length has to get physically close to a small subject to fill the frame at 1:1; a longer focal length can stand back and still reach the same ratio. This matters enormously in the field: more working distance means less risk of your own shadow falling on the subject, more room for a flash or diffuser between you and what you're shooting, and — for anything that startles easily — a better chance the subject stays put.

<Callout type="tip">
If you're choosing equipment for live subjects (insects, water droplets that evaporate, anything that reacts to a looming lens), prioritize working distance over raw magnification. A lens that reaches 1:1 from a comfortable distance is far more usable in practice than one that reaches higher magnification but forces you within a few centimeters of the subject.
</Callout>

## Depth of Field at Close Range

Depth of field — the zone that appears acceptably sharp — shrinks dramatically as magnification increases, faster than most beginners expect. As a rough rule of thumb, it falls off roughly with the square of magnification: doubling magnification doesn't halve your sharp zone, it can quarter it. At high magnifications, the usable depth of field can be a millimeter or less, even at narrow apertures — the single biggest technical adjustment macro demands relative to any other genre.

The obvious response is to stop down the aperture, but macro adds a complication: as magnification increases, the *effective* aperture darkens beyond what the marked f-number suggests, and diffraction softening arrives earlier than at normal shooting distances. Narrow apertures that would comfortably extend depth of field at portrait or landscape distances can already be visibly softening fine detail once you're working near 1:1.

<Callout type="warning">
Don't chase depth of field by stopping down indefinitely. Past a certain point at high magnification, a narrower aperture buys you a slightly larger sharp zone at the cost of overall image sharpness from diffraction — the fix stops helping and starts hurting. If you need more depth of field than one frame at a reasonable aperture can deliver, that's a signal to stack multiple frames rather than close the aperture further.
</Callout>

## Lighting

Close-range work changes lighting in two ways: light output falls off quickly over short distances, and the working space between camera and subject is often obstructed by the lens itself, so light from behind the camera gets partially blocked before it reaches the subject. Small, purpose-built light sources positioned close to the subject — ring lights, small panels, off-camera flashes with diffusion — solve both problems: enough light, close enough, angled around the lens barrel rather than through it.

Diffusion matters more than raw output. A small light source held close to a small subject creates a hard, contrasty shadow that can obscure the texture you're trying to show. Softening that light — with a diffuser panel, translucent material, or a bounce surface — spreads it into a broader source relative to the subject's size, which is what actually controls shadow softness.

## Focus Stacking

Because depth of field at high magnification is so thin, a single frame often can't hold an entire three-dimensional subject in focus without stopping down far enough to introduce diffraction softening. Focus stacking sidesteps that trade-off entirely: instead of one frame doing all the work, you shoot a series of frames at the same framing and exposure, each with the focus point shifted slightly deeper into the subject, then blend the sharpest regions of each frame together in post-processing. The result is a composite with depth of field far beyond what any single exposure at a sensible aperture could achieve — sharp from front to back at a magnification where that would otherwise be impossible.

The two variables that determine a stack's success are the number of frames and the focus increment between them — how far the focus point moves from one frame to the next. Too large an increment leaves gaps of unblended blur between frames; too small wastes frames and shooting time without adding sharpness. Both depend on the aperture and magnification you're shooting at, which is exactly the kind of calculation worth letting a tool handle rather than guessing in the field.

## Getting Started Checklist

You don't need a large kit to start. A short, deliberate list will get you further than buying broadly:

- **Reach 1:1 with something** — a dedicated macro lens, extension tubes on an existing lens, or a close-up attachment filter. Any of these is a legitimate entry point; the magnification ratio matters more than which method gets you there.
- **A stable base.** Depth of field this thin makes hand-holding shake far more visible than at normal distances. A tripod, or at minimum careful bracing, pays off immediately.
- **A small, diffused light source.** Prioritize control and softness over raw power — you're lighting something a few centimeters across.
- **A way to check focus precisely.** At high magnification, autofocus can hunt or lock onto the wrong plane; manual focus with focus peaking or magnified live view gives you the control this genre demands.
- **A plan for focus stacking.** Even a handful of frames per subject, shifted incrementally, will rescue shots that a single exposure can't hold together.

Start with static, cooperative subjects — small objects, plant detail, anything that won't move — before working up to live insects or anything time-pressured. The technical fundamentals above stay identical either way; only your margin for error changes.
