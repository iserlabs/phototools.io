## Magnification Comes First

Getting started in macro photography takes three things: a way to reach high magnification with the camera you already own, a grasp of why depth of field collapses at close range, and a plan for the frames where a single exposure cannot hold the whole subject. Gear is the least of the three. Extension tubes on an ordinary 50mm lens teach the same physics as a dedicated macro lens, because everything difficult about this genre traces back to one number.

Magnification is the ratio of the subject's size on the sensor to its size in real life. At 1:1, usually called life-size or true macro, a subject 10mm across projects a 10mm image onto the sensor. A full-frame sensor at 1:1 therefore sees a patch of the world 36 x 24mm; an APS-C sensor sees about 24 x 16mm. Plenty of lenses sold with "macro" on the barrel stop at 1:2 or less, which is close-up work rather than macro reproduction. There is nothing wrong with starting there, but the ratio on the spec sheet tells you what the lens does. The name on the barrel doesn't.

One consequence of the definition catches nearly everyone at some point: backing off to a lower magnification can never make a small subject fill more of the frame. Magnification is the frame fill. A 6mm subject at 1:1 spans 6mm on the sensor, about a quarter of the height of a full-frame image; filling the 24mm short side takes 4x. When a subject looks too small in the viewfinder, the choices are more magnification or a crop in post. Shooting looser and hoping is not one of them.

## Working Distance Decides What You Can Shoot

Working distance is the gap between the front of the lens and the subject at a given magnification. It is related to focal length but not the same thing. Of two lenses that both reach 1:1, the longer one gets there from farther away: a 50mm macro at life-size may put its front element within a few centimeters of the subject, a 100mm design does the same job from roughly twice as far, and lenses in the 150 to 200mm class from farther still.

That gap shapes the whole session. More working distance leaves room to fit a light or a diffuser between lens and subject, keeps your own shadow out of the picture, and gives anything alive less reason to bolt.

<Callout type="tip">
If live subjects are the goal, weight working distance above maximum magnification when choosing equipment. A lens that reaches 1:1 from 15cm is more useful in practice than one that reaches 2x from 4cm, because at 4cm most insects have already left.
</Callout>

## Depth of Field at Close Range

Depth of field, the zone that renders acceptably sharp, shrinks roughly with the square of magnification. Double the magnification and the sharp zone drops to around a quarter. At 1:1 and a marked f/8, a full-frame camera holds about one millimeter of the subject in focus by ordinary print standards. At twice life-size, a fraction of that. No other genre asks you to work inside tolerances this tight, and adjusting to them is most of what learning macro means.

There is a subtlety inside "acceptably sharp" that explains a lot of confusing results. Every depth of field figure depends on a blur threshold called the circle of confusion: the largest blur you are willing to call sharp. The conventional full-frame value, about 0.03mm, assumes a modest print viewed at arm's length. A single pixel on a modern high-resolution sensor is closer to 0.004mm. Both are legitimate, and they answer different questions. Scroll around a file at 100% and you are judging by the strict standard, where the real sharp zone is several times shallower than the number a calculator gave you under the loose one. The same capture can pass as a print and fail on screen. When a macro shot "should" have carried enough depth and didn't, a mismatched sharpness standard is a more common culprit than a bad lens.

## Effective Aperture and the Diffraction Ceiling

At close range, the aperture doing the work is not the one marked on the lens. Effective aperture equals the marked value multiplied by one plus the magnification. A marked f/8 at 1:1 behaves like f/16; a marked f/2.8 at 2x is working at roughly f/8.4. Two things follow, and both arrive earlier than the marked numbers suggest.

The first is light loss. At life-size the effective aperture costs two stops of exposure compared with the same lens focused at normal distance, and at 2x the penalty passes three stops. This is why macro exposures run long at apertures that sound bright, and why added light is standard practice in this genre rather than an accessory.

The second is diffraction. Light passing a small opening spreads, and past a certain effective aperture that spread softens fine detail across the whole frame no matter how good the glass is. Because effective aperture climbs with magnification, diffraction arrives at marked apertures that look conservative. A lens set to a marked f/11 at life-size is really working at f/22, which is visibly soft territory on any modern high-resolution sensor.

Do not buy depth of field by stopping down without limit. At high magnification each further stop adds a sliver of sharp zone and softens the entire frame in exchange, and past the diffraction threshold the trade stops paying. If one frame at a sensible aperture cannot cover the subject, the fix is focus stacking rather than a smaller aperture.
</Callout>

## Diagnosing a Soft Macro Shot

Soft macro frames have a short list of causes, and each one leaves a different fingerprint. Checking in order saves sessions.

Look for direction first. Blur that smears one way, or shows doubled edges, is motion: a shutter speed too long for the setup, or vibration reaching the camera. Next, look for a sharp plane. If one band of the subject is crisp while material right beside it is mush, that is depth of field. The sharp zone was thinner than the subject, and no amount of blaming the lens changes it. If nothing anywhere in the frame is crisp, and stopping down made things worse rather than better, that is diffraction.

The common trap is calling a depth problem diffraction, or a bad lens, because the marked aperture sounded safe. It rarely is at these magnifications. Sharpness that varies across a subject sitting at the same distance from the camera is depth. Nothing else behaves that way.

## Focus Stacking, and When to Skip It

Focus stacking answers the depth problem directly. Shoot a series of frames with identical framing and exposure, shifting focus a small step deeper into the subject each time, then let stacking software blend the sharpest region of each frame into one composite. The result carries depth of field no single exposure at a sensible aperture could deliver.

Whether you need it is a three-question check, in order. Is the subject genuinely flat and parallel to the sensor? Then one frame covers it, and if that frame comes back soft the cause is nearly always tilt rather than depth. At high magnification a flat subject a degree off parallel can already run out of the sharp zone, so re-level and reshoot rather than adding frames. Does the subject's depth barely exceed one frame's sharp zone? Then one stop down covers it more cheaply than a stack. Only real, unavoidable depth justifies the full series.

A stack has two variables: the focus step between frames and the number of frames. The step must be a little smaller than the sharp depth of a single frame, around 70 to 80 percent of it, so the zones overlap with no bands of blur between them. The frame count is the subject's depth divided by that step, plus two or three frames beyond sharp at each end, because blending software behaves better with some run-out. Both numbers move with aperture and magnification, which makes this a calculation worth doing before the session instead of a guess during it.

Lock exposure and white balance across the whole series. Any drift between frames shows up as banding or flicker in the blend, and it stays invisible until the merge is done.

## Lighting at Macro Distances

Close range changes lighting twice over. Output falls off quickly across small distances, and the lens itself occupies much of the space the light would otherwise travel through, so illumination from behind the camera arrives partly blocked. The practical answer is small sources positioned close to the subject and angled around the lens barrel: a compact flash with a diffuser, a small panel, a ring light.

Diffusion matters more than power. Shadow softness is controlled by the size of the light source relative to the subject, and a bare flash a few centimeters from a beetle is still a hard point source. Softening it through translucent material, or bouncing it off a nearby white card, turns it into something large relative to a small subject. That is the whole trick.

One less obvious enemy is stray light striking the front element. When you shoot toward a bright background or under strong room light, veiling flare lifts the shadows and eats micro-contrast, and it reads as an unsharp image long before it reads as a foggy one. If a well-focused frame still looks lifeless, shade the lens before blaming it.

## A Sensible Way to Begin

Three routes reach 1:1, and all of them count. A dedicated macro lens is the simplest and keeps focus available from infinity down to life-size. Extension tubes move an existing lens closer than it was designed to focus; they are cheap, contain no glass to degrade the image, and cost you distant focus while mounted. Close-up filters that thread onto the front are the cheapest entry, with quality that varies by design. The magnification you reach matters far more than the route that got you there.

Beyond that, a short list beats a shopping spree: some form of stable support, because at these magnifications camera shake displaces the framing by whole subject-widths; a small light with diffusion; and manual focus with magnified live view or focus peaking, since autofocus tends to hunt at high magnification or lock onto the wrong plane.

<Callout type="tip">
Most setups never record magnification, yet it sets your effective aperture, depth of field, and framing. Photograph a ruler once per configuration and note how many millimeters span the frame: sensor width divided by that measurement is the magnification. A full-frame sensor showing 12mm across the frame is at 3x. One test frame, and the number is yours for good.
</Callout>

Start on static subjects. Coins, fabric weave, plant detail, crystals, anything that holds still while you work out focus steps and lighting. The physics stays identical when you graduate to insects and other subjects on a clock. What shrinks is your margin for error.
